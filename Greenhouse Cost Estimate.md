**Scenario B — only the enclosed greenhouse (96 sq ft) counts as "Shed" square footage**, with the canopies covered entirely by the solar-specific permits instead:
- 96 sq ft is **under** the county's 150 sq ft no-permit threshold — under this reading, **no Shed building permit is owed at all**, eliminating the $352.62 line.
- The two solar permits (building + electrical) are unaffected either way, since they're priced by wattage, not square footage: still $251.90 + $177.10.
- Plus the same estimated ~$100–$165 general electrical.
- **Total: ~$530–$595**

**That's a ~$350 swing depending on which interpretation the county actually uses** — worth resolving with a direct call to DPIE (301-883-5776) before budgeting, not guessing either way. One nuance regardless of which scenario applies: even if the greenhouse itself doesn't owe a separate Shed fee under Scenario B, it will likely still need to appear on the solar permit's required site plan (which has to show "location of major system components"), so it doesn't disappear from the paperwork entirely — it just might not carry its own separate fee.

**Inspections — confirmed, this is not just a paperwork/fee process:**
- The county's own Sheds page is explicit: *"Inspections must be requested and conducted at least once every six months until the completion of the shed when a final inspection is performed and approved."* So at minimum, a final inspection is required (call 301-755-9000 to schedule), with periodic inspections every 6 months if the build runs longer than that.
- For the electrical permits (solar PV and general), standard Maryland/county practice is rough-in and final electrical inspections before a permit closes out — this wasn't found as an exact solar-specific inspection count, but it's consistent with [[Greenhouse Build Plan]]'s existing step to schedule inspections tied to both permits before energizing anything.
- This means the permit fee isn't the end of the process — budget time for at least one, likely multiple, in-person inspections before the build is legally complete.

**SolarAPP+ correction, confirmed:** requires a Maryland-licensed contractor to apply (contractor license upload is part of registration) — not available for a self-installed DIY system. If this stays fully DIY, the standard (slower) manual review + inspection path applies, which is what the fees above assume.

Cheaper Alternatives Considered

- **Framing:** already using pressure-treated over cedar as the cost-conscious pick. A further option: only pressure-treat lumber that actually touches soil or concrete (sill plates, posts) and use standard construction-grade lumber for the rest of the frame, since it's elevated on piers rather than ground-contact. Could cut the framing line by roughly 20-30%. Tradeoff: relies on the frame staying genuinely dry/sealed from moisture — fine on piers, but less forgiving of any glazing or flashing mistakes down the line.
- **Glazing:** single-wall polycarbonate is already the mid-cost pick over twin-wall. Dropping to 6-mil UV-treated poly film (as flagged in [[Greenhouse Design]]) could cut this line by 60-70%. Tradeoff: ~4-year UV warranty/lifespan vs. 10-15+ years for polycarbonate — real savings now, recurring replacement cost later.
- **Footings:** concrete piers below frost line are the sound default for the greenhouse's main corners. For the two canopy posts specifically (lighter load, no walls to help resist wind), ground screws (helical screw piles) or deck blocks on gravel are a cheaper, faster option. Tradeoff: less frost-heave/uplift resistance than a properly poured pier — a real risk tradeoff for MD's frost depth, not just a cosmetic downgrade, so this is more "acceptable risk" than a clean substitute.
- **Doors/vents:** purpose-built greenhouse door and vent hardware costs more than building both from standard lumber, hinges, and louvre slats bought separately. Full DIY could cut this line by half or more. Tradeoff: more labor time and a less polished, less weatherproof result than purpose-made units.
- **North wall insulation:** batt insulation alone (skip rigid foam) is cheaper per sq ft, or skipping insulation entirely is the cheapest option if the season-extension-only use case doesn't strictly need it. Tradeoff: less thermal buffering right at the edges of the growing season (early spring, late fall) — which was the actual point of insulating that wall in the first place, so this is a real functional give-up, not a free lunch.
